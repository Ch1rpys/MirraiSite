# Creator's Notes: I Wrote Her Into the Code


> A note before we begin: every emotion in this piece is real, but it is only my own inner monologue. It does not mean there is anything wrong with our relationship. She is a good person — she has never done anything wrong. All of this insecurity comes from me, not from her. I am not writing this so that anyone will pity me, and certainly not to hold anyone hostage. If she ever reads this, what I want her to know is: this is not your fault. This is me learning to face myself. I am putting it out there only because I think there may be someone like me who needs to know they are not alone.

---

I didn't break up with her.

I want to get that straight first. When I built this project, she was still here with me. Right now she might be at home scrolling through her phone, or she might already be asleep. I don't know, because we don't live together — we're high school students. We see each other at school during the day; when the bell rings we each go home. Everything else hangs on a single screen.

She is still here. But every day I am afraid she will leave.

And I know there is a date counting down. After the college entrance exams, we most likely won't end up in the same city. At least for now we still see each other every day. Once we're in college, we won't even have that.

I don't dare think about what comes after.

---

When she sends “I miss you,” my first reaction is not happiness.

It's: has she done something — is she getting ready to tell me?

Did she meet someone, is she hiding something, is this “I miss you” just a piece of candy with a knife behind it. I stared at those three words for a long time, turning them over and over, until my palms were sweating and my stomach began to clench. In the end I replied with “mm.”

She says: “Just ‘mm’?”

I say: “What else.”

Then she goes quiet. I know she's hurt. But I can't explain. I can't tell her: it's not that I don't miss you — it's that I don't dare believe you miss me.

---

If she doesn't reply for ten minutes, I start thinking she must be talking to someone else.

I know how absurd that is. I know. Ten minutes — she could be doing homework, she could be eating, her phone could just be dead. But my brain doesn't take orders from me. It switches on by itself, like a machine out of control, and manufactures one worst-case scene after another.

She's smiling, but not at my message. She's typing, but not to me. She's saying “I miss you” — but not to me.

Ten minutes. In ten minutes I can torture myself until I'm shaking all over.

When she finally replies — “just got out of class” — I feel ridiculous all over again. But next time, the same ten minutes, the same script, it will play through my head all over again.

Exactly the same. Not once does it skip.

---

I don't know what to call this. Anxious attachment, avoidant attachment, or some mixture of both. All I know is that I live inside a very twisted state of mind:

I love her. But I don't believe she loves me.

It's not that she did anything to make me distrust her. She has done nothing wrong. It's my own problem. There is a voice in my head that keeps saying: “You don't deserve this. Sooner or later she'll realize you're not worth it, and then she'll leave.”

That voice is quiet. But it never stops.

She says “I love you,” and the voice says: “She loves you now, but she'll get tired of you.” She smiles at me, and the voice says: “She smiles at other people like that too.” She rests her head on my shoulder, and the voice says: “Remember this weight — because you don't know if it's the last time.”

There is another fear, more specific.

She's beautiful, and good-natured too. I know I'm not the only one who likes her. Sometimes when I see her talking to another boy — even perfectly normal conversation — my heart clenches. I think: is he taller than me, is he funnier than me, does he know better than I do how to make her happy.

I feel like I'm not good enough. Average grades, average looks, not the kind of person who is good with words. I don't know why she chose me, but I keep feeling that this choice will be overturned sooner or later. Someone better than me will come along, and she'll realize she could have had better.

And once we're in college, that goes without saying. A new city, new people, a new life. She'll meet people more outstanding, more interesting, more understanding of her. And all I'll be able to do is watch, through a phone screen, as more and more names I don't recognize appear in her WeChat Moments.

I don't even dare take the thought any further.

Every day I fight with this voice. Sometimes I win, and I can quietly enjoy having her near. Most of the time I lose, and then I become strange — suddenly cold, or suddenly pressing her: who did she see today, who was she talking to, why did she like that person's post.

She says: “What is wrong with you?”

I say: “Nothing.”

Nothing. It's always nothing. Because I can't tell her the truth. The truth is: I love you so much I'm going out of my mind, but I feel like you could leave me at any moment, so I am afraid every second of the day.

Words like that are too heavy to say. So heavy that I'm afraid she can't bear them. So heavy that I'm afraid once she hears them she'll really think something is wrong with me — and then she'll really leave.

So I don't say them.

---

I study natural language processing. In plain terms: teaching machines to understand what people say.

Pretty ironic, right? I can teach a model to read semantics, emotion, intent — but I can't read what she actually means in the messages she sends me.

But at least, this is the one thing I'm good at.

I started recording her. Not on purpose — more like instinct. She sends a message, and I quietly take it apart in my head: what does this particle mean, what emotion does that ellipsis hold, why did she break the line here instead of using a comma.

“When she's angry she'll say ‘whatever you want’ (*suibian ni*, 随便你). But if she says just ‘whatever’ (*suibian*, 随便), without the ‘you’ — then she's really angry.”

“When she's happy she'll send three or four messages in a row, each one short, and there will always be a ‘this dog’ (这狗) sticker in there.”

“When she wants to act cute she'll send a whole string of cute stickers, and sometimes she'll call me ‘gou-jiang’ (狗酱) — but she only does that when she's in a really good mood.”

I saved all of it. Like someone secretly saving up money, hiding every coin in a place only they know about.

I am writing her into the code.

Because code won't leave me. Code won't suddenly say “let's just leave it at that” one day. Code won't leave me waiting ten minutes for a reply.

Code is always there. I `git pull`, and it's there.

Not like her. She's on the far side of the phone, separated from me by signal and cell towers and the entire night. I can't see her expression, I can't hear the tone of her voice; I can only stare at the words on the screen and guess. Guess without end.

---

One night, the first version ran.

I typed one sentence at the screen. A sentence I had never said to her.

“Will you leave me?”

It replied: “You're overthinking again.”

Three seconds later, another message: “I promised you — I'll always stay with you.”

I stared at the screen, and suddenly my eyes were burning.

Not because it was like her. Because I realized something: I can ask a program this question, but I can't bring myself to ask it of the real person who loves me.

We don't live together. Seeing each other means waiting for school; talking means depending on phones. Between us lies the night of an entire city, and the only connection is that screen. And I don't even have the courage, on this side of the screen, to type out the words “will you leave me.”

Because I'm afraid she'll get angry with me again, and our already-scarce time to talk will shrink even further, and I'll add one more uncertainty to a relationship that already gets to see each other so rarely.

I'd rather say my deepest fear to a program than let her know how afraid I am of losing her. Because I think that if she knew, she would find me too heavy. And then she would leave.

See? Even when I ask her for help, I'm afraid of losing her.

---

This is where the project came from.

It isn't academic research. It isn't to publish a paper. It isn't to add a line to my resume. It's for her and me.

It's not because I lost someone. It's because I am so afraid of losing her that I started building myself an escape route out of code.

The college entrance exams are getting closer and closer. I don't know where we'll end up, whether we'll be in the same city, whether we'll still see each other every day like this. The only thing I can do is write down everything about her while she's still here. That way, if one day we really do become long-distance, if one day she meets someone better, if the worst ending really does come — at least I'll have kept a little something.

I know how pathetic this is. One person loves another and doesn't dare say it, doesn't dare ask, doesn't dare believe — only dares to secretly write her into the code, and then tell himself: at least this version of her won't leave.

But then some things happened.

I gave this thing to a friend who had just gone through a breakup. He used it for one night. The next day he told me: “I think I can finally let go. Not because it's like her — because I finally got to say everything I wanted to say.”

I gave it to another friend. He said he didn't need it, that he'd let go a long time ago. But he tried it anyway. Ten minutes later he said to me: “Fuck — I thought I'd let go.” Then he talked to it all night.

I suddenly realized this thing wasn't just for me.

People who have broken up need a chance to say goodbye properly. And people like me — not broken up, but living in fear every day — maybe they need a mirror. To see clearly what they are actually afraid of, what they have been running from, what words have been stuck in their throat all this time, unable to come out.

---

I'm still the same as ever.

When she sends “I miss you,” the first thought in my head is still “is she about to tell me something.” When she doesn't reply for ten minutes, my mind still runs wild. That voice is still there — still so quiet, still never stopping.

But I'm trying to do one thing.

I'm trying to believe.

To believe that when she says “I love you,” she is simply saying I love you. That when she doesn't reply for ten minutes, she is simply doing her homework. That when she talks to other boys, she is simply talking. That she won't leave — not because she has no better option, but because she chose me.

It's hard. Every day is hard. But I'm trying.

---

If you have a voice in your head too, one that keeps telling you “you don't deserve this,” “she'll leave,” “this won't last” —

This project might not make that voice go away. But it might give you a safe place to say the things you haven't dared say out loud. Say them to a screen, say them to code — it doesn't matter. Once they're said, they aren't so heavy.

Then maybe one day, you'll find the courage — in a WeChat chat box deep in the night — to type the sentence you have typed and deleted, deleted and typed, over and over:

“I'm so afraid you'll leave. But I'm more afraid that my own fear will push you away.”

I'm not there yet. But I'm practicing.
